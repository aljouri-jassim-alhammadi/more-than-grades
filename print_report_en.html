1.4 Prompt Engineering Log: Actual Prompts & Iterative Refinement

To demonstrate prompt evolution, the following case studies document the initial prompts, the reasons for modification, and the finalized prompts:

Case 1: Story Generation (Hamad's Exam Anxiety)

Initial Prompt: "Write a story about a high school student stressed about grades."

Why & How It Was Changed: The initial prompt produced a generic American high school story about SAT exams and lockers. It did not match UAE university culture and offered no real solutions. I modified the prompt by specifying the setting (an engineering student in Abu Dhabi), adding realistic peer conversations, and requiring practical stress-management tools.

Final Prompt: "Write an empathetic 3-part story about Hamad, a university engineering student in Abu Dhabi feeling overwhelmed by midterm exam stress. Show a conversation with his friend Sultan that helps him realize his grade does not measure his self-worth, and integrate the Pomodoro focus technique and 4-7-8 breathing exercises as practical coping steps."

Case 2: Brand Logo Symbol

Initial Prompt: "A logo for a student grades website."

Why & How It Was Changed: The first prompt generated simple 'A+' letter grades and red grading pencils. This contradicted our message that students are more than grades. I changed the prompt to focus on an inspiring metaphor of growth, flight, and human potential using UAE-inspired falcon symbolism.

Final Prompt: "A minimalist modern vector logo icon: an open textbook smoothly transforming into soaring falcon wings, with a glowing central human figure rising upward. Emerald teal and warm sunset coral gradients, dark slate circular badge background, flat vector style, no letters or numbers."

Case 3: Visual Scenarios & Character Artwork

Initial Prompt: "Students studying together in class."

Why & How It Was Changed: The output was a dark, generic stock photo with foreign styling. I revised the prompt by specifying camera details (35mm lens, f/1.8 aperture), warm afternoon sunlight, modern architectural glass features, and modest campus attire suitable for UAE universities.

Final Prompt: "Cinematic 35mm photograph of two diverse university students sitting together in a bright, modern campus courtyard in Dubai, one student encouraging their quiet friend who looks stressed over notebooks, warm golden hour lighting, respectful modest student clothing, photorealistic, 4k."

Case 4: 35-Second Homepage Narrative Video & Voiceover

Initial Prompt: "Make a short video about exam pressure."

Why & How It Was Changed: The initial video had erratic scene transitions and distorted face morphing. I structured the prompt into four clear narrative steps with consistent camera movement and added synchronized English audio narration.

Final Prompt: "A 35-second 4-part video storyboard: 1) Clock ticking over an exam paper; 2) Student looking overwhelmed in a campus hallway; 3) Supportive classmate placing a comforting hand on their shoulder; 4) Students smiling outdoors showing coding and art projects. Calm pacing, cinematic lighting, with clear English narration: 'Your grade is part of your journey, not the measure of your worth'."

Part 1 – Process Analysis: Design, Development, and Personal Experience

Working on the 'More Than Grades' project has been a valuable learning experience that changed the way I look at digital media creation. Before this project, I used to think of artificial intelligence as a tool where you just type a simple question and get a finished product immediately. However, I quickly learned that using AI effectively is like acting as a creative director. You need to guide the tools step by step, give clear instructions, and carefully review and edit every single output.

In this project, I used different AI tools for specific parts of the website. For the written content, I used ChatGPT and Claude to brainstorm ideas and draft the initial stories for our fictional student characters—Rashid, Mariam, and Hamad. While the AI generated the first drafts in seconds, I noticed that the stories often felt generic or sounded like they took place in a foreign school. I had to manually edit the dialogue, add local campus context, and introduce practical student advice like the Pomodoro focus method and breathing exercises.

For the visual imagery, I used Midjourney and DALL-E to create the 24 illustrations across all eight pages. This was one of the most eye-opening parts of the project. When I started with simple prompts, the AI produced Western-style classrooms that did not feel authentic to students in the UAE. Through multiple rounds of testing, I learned to add specific details to my prompts—such as modern glass university architecture, warm afternoon sunlight, and modest student attire. This taught me that prompt writing requires patience and careful observation.

For the video and audio on the homepage, I used Runway Gen-3 to generate the 35-second visual sequence, and Web Audio tools to create the synchronized English voiceover narration. The biggest challenge here was keeping the character appearance consistent between scene cuts. I solved this by dividing the video into four clear storyboard steps: starting with exam stress, moving to peer support, and ending with students smiling and sharing their creative hobbies.

Designing the website itself taught me practical technical skills. I chose a dark theme with calming emerald teal and warm sunset coral because these colors feel modern, welcoming, and easy on the eyes. I also added a fast search tool and an anonymous contact form so students can explore advice or reach out privately without fear. One technical challenge happened when we deployed the site to GitHub Pages: two images did not show up because of an unescaped symbol in the SVG code. Fixing that taught me that building a good website requires testing everything on live browsers rather than assuming it will work automatically.

Looking back at the finished platform, I am proud of how empathetic and supportive the website feels. If I had more time, I would like to add an interactive student discussion forum and short podcast interviews with real university counselors.

Part 2 – Ethical Considerations: Synthetic Personas & Algorithmic Accountability

Using artificial intelligence to create media about student wellbeing brings important ethical responsibilities. In this project, two ethical issues stood out: the risks of Deepfake Technology and the need for Algorithmic Accountability.

First, deepfake technology has advanced so rapidly that it is now easy to create realistic synthetic humans and voices (Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020). While we used AI-generated characters like Sultan and Noura to tell encouraging stories, the same technology can be used negatively—such as creating fake videos for cyberbullying or spreading false rumors (Floridi, 2021). An important ethical boundary in educational media is making sure students are not deceived. Distressed students should never be misled into thinking an AI character is a licensed human therapist. To be honest and transparent, our website includes clear disclosure badges on every page stating that all characters and stories are created with AI.

Second, algorithmic accountability means that as media creators, we are personally responsible for the content we publish, even if an AI helped write it (Diakopoulos, 2019). AI models learn from existing internet data, which often contains hidden cultural biases and stereotypes (Jobin et al., 2019). During my work, I had to reject several AI image results that portrayed students in stereotyped or inaccurate ways. In line with the UAE AI Ethics Guide (2022), I made sure all visual and written content treated student experiences with respect, dignity, and cultural sensitivity.

Part 3 – Societal Implications: The Digital Advertising & Social Campaigns Industry

The rise of generative artificial intelligence is having a major impact on the Digital Advertising and Social Campaigns industry across three important areas: Creativity, Employment, and Trust.

Regarding Creativity, AI tools have made it much faster and cheaper for small teams and non-profit organizations to create professional-looking social campaigns (Davenport et al., 2020). Instead of needing a large production studio, an individual can design high-quality campaign posters and video concepts in a single afternoon. However, there is a creative downside: because AI models predict words and images based on past trends, campaigns risk looking repetitive and generic if creators rely only on default prompts without adding original human insight.

Regarding Employment, the advertising industry is seeing significant changes in job roles. Basic entry-level tasks—such as creating simple draft banners or writing standard ad copy—are increasingly automated (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2017). This does not mean creative jobs will disappear, but workers will need new skills. The industry now values creative editors, prompt writers, and cultural strategists who can guide AI tools and verify their accuracy (World Economic Forum, 2023).

Finally, Trust is the most critical factor in advertising and social campaigns. When audiences realize that images, customer reviews, or brand messages are created by AI, they can easily become skeptical (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). In awareness campaigns—especially those dealing with sensitive youth topics like anti-bullying and mental health—audiences must trust the message. If students feel a campaign is purely synthetic marketing with no real empathy behind it, the message will fail. Long-term trust will depend on full transparency and showing audiences that AI is used to support genuine human communication, not replace it.
